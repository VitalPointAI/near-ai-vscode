# J6 (C4, communications systems and cyberspace) agent team: doctrine research (US, Canadian, NATO)

See design note 10, sections 2.6, 2.8 and 2.9. JP 3-14 (23 August 2023) has now been read from the owner's extract; its SATCOM and PNT mission areas match the touchpoints below.

## Summary (key points first)

1. **The J6 builds and runs the commander's communications system and plans its use; it does not own the operations it enables.** In US joint doctrine the J-6 publishes communications system plans, annexes and operating instructions, provides overall management of the communications system, reviews subordinate plans and provides for interoperability. As forces deploy it establishes a joint network operations control center (JNCC), its "operational arm", which serves as the single control agency for the joint communications networks. [S1 II-11, III-1 to III-3] HIGH
2. **The J6's core planning product is the communications system estimate, and its core order product is the communications annex.** JP 6-0 calls the estimate "the J-6's assessment of COAs" and divides communications planning into five areas: mission analysis, information requirements analysis, interoperability and supportability analysis, capability analysis, and allocation of assets. The estimate ends with a plain statement of whether the mission is supportable and which COA is best supported. [S1 III-5, III-15 to III-16, B-1 to B-8] HIGH
3. **Annex letters differ by doctrine, and the template must switch them.** US joint: Annex K, titled "Command, Control, Communications, and Computer (C4) Systems" in JP 5-0 (JP 3-33 calls it "Communications Systems"). US Army: Annex H (Signal), written by the G-6 or S-6. Canada (CFJP 5.0) and NATO (AJP-6): Annex Q, Communications and Information Systems. Cyberspace guidance sits in an appendix to Annex C (Operations): Appendix 16 in joint plans, Appendix 12 (Cyberspace Electromagnetic Activities) in Army orders. [S5; S4 X-1 to X-2; S7 2-68; S10 Appendix E; S11 5E1-2; S12 3.3c; S1 III-6; S6 B-34] HIGH
4. **Offensive cyber is integrated through operations, not through the J6.** The cyberspace operations appendix is part of Annex C (Operations); the combatant commander's cyberspace operations planning staff plans DODIN operations, defensive and offensive cyberspace operations; JP 3-12's lead agent is the Joint Staff J-39 (Global Operations); and at Army brigade and higher the cyber electromagnetic warfare officer, coordinated by the S-3 or fires, writes the cyber appendix. The J6 owns DODIN operations and contributes network dependencies and key terrain. In NATO, any offensive cyberspace operation goes through the Sovereign Cyber Effects Provided Voluntarily by Allies (SCEPVA) mechanism, and command of the forces stays national. The template therefore has no offensive cyber member. [S1 III-3 to III-4, III-6; S2 III-6, appendix D; S7 2-90 to 2-91; S13 2.23 and chapter 1 section 3 p. 11] HIGH for the doctrine; "through the J3" is a fair summary (MEDIUM, inference from annex ownership).
5. **Cyber terms must be used exactly.** JP 3-12 defines three missions: DODIN operations (secure, configure, operate, extend, maintain and sustain DoD cyberspace), defensive cyberspace operations (DCO) and offensive cyberspace operations (OCO). Doctrine separates "cyberspace security" (a tactical action) from the policy term "cybersecurity". The public JP 3-12 is the 8 June 2018 edition; the December 2022 revision is unclassified but available only with a DoD common access card. [S2 II-2, II-5 to II-6; S27; S39] HIGH
6. **Spectrum is shared work, and the JEMSO cell is normally in the J3.** JP 3-85 assigns EMS management to the J-6, normally delegated to the joint frequency management office (JFMO). The joint electromagnetic spectrum operations cell (JEMSOC) "normally resides in the J-3" (it may sit in the J-6 in peacetime), and at JTF level it absorbs the old joint spectrum management element. The joint restricted frequency list (JRFL) combines J-2, J-3 and J-6 inputs. NATO says the same: theatre spectrum management sits inside the J6 under J3 responsibility. [S3 II-5 to II-11, III-8; S12 1.14c to 1.14e] HIGH
7. **PACE is Army doctrine with a joint echo, and it may be the most valuable thing an S6 produces.** FM 6-02 says "A viable, effective PACE plan may be the most valuable contribution of a G-6 (S-6)". Each PACE plan must be feasible, acceptable, suitable, distinguishable and complete; units normally keep one to higher and one to subordinates; plans are set per phase and per warfighting function and are rehearsed with triggers. FM 3-0 (2022 and 2025) repeats the requirement for rehearsed PACE plans and an analog COP. JP 6-0 asks for primary, secondary and tertiary means in multinational planning. [S6 1-50 to 1-52, B-6 to B-8; S8 3-54; S9 3-58, 8-24; S1 III-12] HIGH
8. **Authorization of networks is a named human decision.** The J-6 ensures every network is accredited and "may be the authorizing official" for theatre networks, under DoDI 8510.01 (Risk Management Framework). Canada uses ITSG-33. An agent can assemble and check the package; it cannot authorize, assign frequencies, release COMSEC to a partner, or approve a cyber operation. [S1 III-6; S24; S25; S1 III-12] HIGH
9. **Canada: no public CFJP 6.0 was found; the public picture is organizational.** CAF Cyber Command (established 26 September 2024) is the single DND/CAF authority for cyber operations and leads cyber operations, joint electronic warfare and SIGINT; its Canadian Forces Network Operations Centre does enterprise cyber defence. Offensive cyber operations are "approved by the Government on a mission-by-mission basis". Deployed task force CIS comes from the Canadian Forces Joint Signal Regiment under the Canadian Forces Joint Operational Support Group (CJOC). CFJP 5.0 has each J branch, including the J6, do its own staff analysis, and puts CIS in Annex Q. [S14; S15; S16; S17; S20; S11 3-12, 5E1-2] HIGH for what is stated; the absence of CFJP 6.0 is a search result (MEDIUM).
10. **Proposed team: 10 members, inside the 6 to 10 asked for and the extension's 12 limit.** A J6 planning lead, network planner, spectrum manager, cyber defense planner, risk and authorization officer, C2 systems integrator, COMSEC coordinator (unclassified scheduling only), information and knowledge management liaison, JNCC watch officer for execution, and an independent J6 checker. The human J6 director, the authorizing official, the COMSEC account manager, the JEMSOC director and the commander stay human. [design recommendation built on S1, S3, S4, S6, S7]
11. **Classification is the main product risk for this team.** Real IP plans, frequency lists, call signs, key change schedules and network diagrams are exactly the material that aggregates into something sensitive. The template must be private by default, run on exercise data, and refuse classified or controlled input. [S36; S37; internal note 05-J5-plans.md section 6]

- **Every product in Table 4 has one drafting owner.** This avoids the Bastion defect of overlapping producers and products with no producer (internal note 00-bastion-staff-teams.md section 6, items 3 and 4). - **Spectrum is one member with a clear boundary.** The JEMSOC and the JRFL belong to the JEMSOC and JFMO; the member drafts J-6 inputs only [S3 II-6, II-8].
- **COMSEC is one small member on a fast model.** Its products are lists and requests; anything involving keying material is outside the extension.
- **The checker reports to the human J6 director, not the lead.** It mirrors the J5 red team design (sibling note 05, Table 5). Its tools exclude writing to product files.
- **Space support is folded into network-planner (SATCOM) and c2-systems-integrator (PNT dependency).** A separate space seat is added only at the strategic profile (see Scaling).

## Details

### 1. US joint doctrine

#### 1.1 JP 6-0 Joint Communications System (2015, Change 1 2019)

- **Purpose of the system.** The communications system supports C2 and must be interoperable, agile, trusted and shared; its functions are acquire, process, store, transport, control and protect [S1 figures I-2, I-3]. Essential services must work under all circumstances, including degraded cyberspace, disconnected, intermittent and limited-bandwidth users, and a nuclear electromagnetic pulse [S1 I-8]. HIGH
- **J-6 duties at a joint force.** Publish communications system plans, annexes and operating instructions; provide overall management; establish a JNCC as forces deploy; review subordinate plans; provide interoperability [S1 II-11 to II-12]. The JNCC "plans and manages the deployed communications system" and controls the joint networks under JFC authority and USCYBERCOM's directive authority for cyberspace operations; subordinate network operations and security centers report through it [S1 III-1, III-3]. HIGH
- **Strategic layer.** The Joint Staff J-6 is the Joint Staff CIO, chairs the MC4EB and is the US member of the Combined Communications-Electronics Board (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, UK, US), which develops Allied Communications Publications used by more than 90 nations [S1 II-7, III-14]. The CCMD J-6 works through the TNCC, which coordinates authorized service interruptions and restorals, develops impact assessments and identifies critical DODIN assets for mission assurance [S1 III-2]. HIGH
- **Planning.** Five areas: mission analysis; information requirements analysis; interoperability, compatibility and supportability analysis; capability analysis; allocation of communications system assets [S1 III-15 to III-16]. Key terrain in cyberspace "is a prerequisite for determining appropriate cyberspace security and cyberspace defense actions within a plan" [S1 III-6]. The J-6 "should obtain a threat summary from the J-2 for threats to communications networks in theater" [S1 III-7]. Appendix B is a full planning guide from situation through wargame to recommendation [S1 B-1 to B-8]. HIGH
- **Orders and authorization.** The primary documents for communications guidance are Appendix 16 (Cyberspace Operations) to Annex C and Annex K. After the plan is approved, the J-6 ensures every network is authorized; it may be the authorizing official for theatre networks and assigns a security control assessor in each component; for other DODIN networks it forwards a consolidated package to the next higher J-6 [S1 III-6]. HIGH
- **Coordination list.** The joint force J-6 keeps close contact with the CCMD J-6, JEMSOC, TNCC, the CO planning staff, the CCMD JFMO, the CCMD COMSEC manager, component operations centers, the DISA liaison, JCSE, partner nations and NGOs [S1 III-6]. This list is a ready-made "handoffs" checklist for j6-lead. HIGH
- **Degraded and denied environments.** Mitigation for loss of bandwidth, connectivity and position, navigation and timing "must be addressed during joint planning"; failures should be transparent to users through automatic transfer to alternate capabilities [S1 II-4 to II-5]. HIGH
- **Execution phases.** Deployment, employment (including network monitoring, control and reporting with performance measures and thresholds), sustainment, transition (branches, sequels, transition plan), termination and redeployment [S1 III-24 to III-30]. HIGH

#### 1.2 JP 3-33 Joint Task Force Headquarters (2018)

The JTF J-6 is the CJTF's principal staff assistant for a "robust, protected, interoperable, and flexible joint communications system" [S4 X-1]. Its notional organization is administration, JNCC, regional space support centers, DISA liaison officer, computer systems support, networks and headquarters support [S4 figure X-2]. Its duties include personnel requirements to the J-1, extra capabilities through the J-3 request-for-forces manager, establishing a JNCC, reviewing component plans, Annex K with preplanned responses for restoration, bandwidth reallocation and intrusion prevention, processing accreditation packages for CJTF approval, cybersecurity procedures with the CCMD J-6 and USCYBERCOM, and assigning trained privileged users [S4 X-3 to X-6]. JCSE provides rapidly deployable early-entry communications for up to four joint force headquarters [S4 X-7]. HIGH (2018 text; see Edition check)

#### 1.3 JP 3-12 Cyberspace Operations (2018 public edition)

- **Missions.** All actions in cyberspace that are not cyberspace-enabled activities fall in one of three missions: OCO, DCO or DODIN operations [S2 executive summary, II-2]. DODIN operations are network-focused and threat-agnostic, include setting up tactical networks and red teaming, and exclude CIO statutory work such as architecture and standards [S2 II-2]. DCO defends the DODIN or other cyberspace DoD forces are ordered to defend, from active threats. OCO projects power in and through foreign cyberspace [S2 executive summary]. HIGH
- **Layers.** Physical network, logical network and cyber-persona layers [S2 I-2 to I-4]. HIGH
- **Authority.** Authorities for specific military cyberspace operations come from SecDef policies and from execute orders and operation orders "authorized by the President or SecDef" [S2 III-1 to III-2]. HIGH
- **Organization.** USCYBERCOM CO-IPEs integrate with each combatant command's CO staff; JFHQ-DODIN coordinates DODIN operations and DCO-IDM globally; JFHQ-Cyber commanders plan and integrate cyberspace missions into combatant command plans [S2 III-6, IV-16 to IV-17]. The publication's lead agent is the Joint Staff J-39 [S2 appendix D, administrative instructions]. HIGH
- **Implication.** The template's cyber member works in the DODIN operations and DCO-IDM lane with the J6; everything else is a handoff to the J3 cyberspace planner and the CO-IPE. MEDIUM (inference from S1 III-3 to III-4 and S2 III-6)

#### 1.4 JP 3-85 Joint Electromagnetic Spectrum Operations (2020)

The J-6 performs EMS management (normally delegated to the JFMO), may be the EMS control authority if designated, designates a JEMSOC core member, identifies network EMS-use requirements for the EMS operating instructions, assists interference resolution and the EMS data repository, negotiates host nation use through the JFMO, and provides communications EMS-use requirements such as SATCOM [S3 II-6]. The JFMO combines J-2, J-3 and J-6 inputs into a proposed JRFL and coordinates allocations [S3 II-8 to II-9]. The JEMSOC "normally resides in the J-3"; at JTF level it subsumes the joint spectrum management element [S3 II-11]. The JEMSOC writes the JEMSO appendix to Annex C after COA selection [S3 III-6]. Components submit EMSO plans including JCEOI elements such as the master net list and call words [S3 II-9]. HIGH

#### 1.5 Annex titles (JP 5-0)

JP 5-0 lists Annex K as "Command, Control, Communications, and Computer (C4) Systems" [S5 annex list]. JP 6-0 uses "Command, Control, Communications, and Computer Systems" [S1 III-6] and JP 3-33 "Communications Systems" [S4 X-2]. The template should print the JP 5-0 title. HIGH

### 2. Cybersecurity, risk management and COMSEC

- **US.** Authorization follows DoDI 8510.01 (19 July 2022), the DoD Risk Management Framework, which assigns responsibilities and procedures for executing and maintaining the RMF [S24; S1 III-6]. The theater army cybersecurity branch "establishes, manages, and assesses" the headquarters cybersecurity program and oversees RMF accreditation [S6 2-50]. MEDIUM (DoDI read through its description) and HIGH (FM 6-02)
- **Canada.** The CAF "holds the responsibility of safeguarding its military networks on a continuous basis", with CSE as the national technical authority for cyber security [S17]. ITSG-33 is the Government of Canada IT security risk management guidance, with departmental and system-level activities [S25]. MEDIUM
- **COMSEC (unclassified level).** In US usage the "COMSEC custodian" is now the COMSEC account manager, responsible for receipt, transfer, accountability, safeguarding and destruction of COMSEC material; the KMI equivalent is the KMI operating account manager [S23]. In Canada, CSE is "the lead security agency and national authority" for COMSEC; departments appoint a Departmental COMSEC Authority; custodians handle generation, receipt, custody, distribution, destruction and accounting; suspected or confirmed incidents are reported within 24 hours through the custodian [S22]. CSE's COMSEC account management course covers custodian duties under ITSD-03A [S38]. Release of US COMSEC to foreign governments is decided by the Committee on National Security Systems before any discussion with foreign nationals [S1 III-12]. HIGH (definitions), MEDIUM (ITSD-03A, from a course description)

### 3. PACE planning

FM 6-02 defines Primary ("the best, and intended, method"), Alternate, Contingency and Emergency ("method of last resort") [S6 1-51]. Rules: two plans (to higher and to subordinates), the higher headquarters usually sets the between-echelon plan, validate in rehearsals and set triggers, and drop any system a subordinate cannot run [S6 1-52]. The five tests are feasible, acceptable, suitable, distinguishable and complete; plans should be simple, built around warfighting functions, set per phase, and published in Annex H; an emergency means may be a procedure such as moving to the last known good point [S6 B-6 to B-8]. FM 3-0 (2022) ties PACE to keeping a digital and an analog COP in large-scale combat [S8 3-54]; FM 3-0 (2025) adds that commanders ensure staffs train on analog C2 and that units rehearse PACE plans, and lists the skills needed under degraded communications [S9 3-58, 8-24 to 8-25]. Joint doctrine uses "primary, secondary, and tertiary means" for multinational services [S1 III-12]. HIGH

### 4. Space support where the J6 touches it

- **SATCOM.** The joint force J-6 validates, consolidates and prioritizes all joint force satellite requests; combatant commands validate and prioritize access requests; CDRUSSPACECOM has operational and configuration authority for DoD SATCOM; satellite channel access and host nation authority to radiate are separate approvals, and missing the second can block the link [S1 III-9 to III-10]. HIGH
- **Liaison.** The JTF J-6 organization includes regional space support centers, and the RSSC liaison officer coordinates ground mobile forces satellite requirements [S4 X-4, X-7]. HIGH
- **PNT.** Loss of position, navigation and timing is a planning mitigation item [S1 II-4]. HIGH
- **Gap.** JP 3-14 was reportedly rewritten in August 2023 [S28] and was not read; Canadian space support to CIS was not researched. LOW

### 5. Multidomain operations (FM 3-0) and networking

FM 3-0 (October 2022) established multidomain operations as the Army's operational concept; the March 2025 edition supersedes it and keeps the concept [S9 foreword]. Networking points for the J6 template: rehearsed PACE plans and an analog COP because communications "are likely to be degraded or denied" [S8 3-54; S9 3-58]; emission control, balancing risk to force against risk to mission, up to communications "with no electromagnetic signature" [S8 3-65]; understanding friendly electromagnetic signatures to assess vulnerabilities [S9 1-111]; and degraded-C2 proficiency (dispersed command posts, analog information management, manual running estimates, command post battle drills), improved through electromagnetic protection [S9 8-24 to 8-25]. At joint level, the public JADC2 strategy summary frames C2 as "sense, make sense, act" and includes a line of effort to modernize mission partner information sharing [S26]. HIGH (FM 3-0), MEDIUM (JADC2 summary read through descriptions)

### 6. US Army G-6 and S-6 (FM 6-02, FM 6-0)

The G-6 or S-6 is the principal staff officer for communications in the area of operations; responsibilities cover DODIN operations and applicable portions of DCO, network transport, information services, spectrum management operations and information management; DCO is coordinated with the CEMA section; the G-6 prepares Annex H [S7 2-67 to 2-68]. The CEWO, at brigade and higher, plans and integrates cyberspace and EW operations and writes Appendix 12 to Annex C, coordinated by the S-3 or fires [S7 2-90 to 2-91]. Corps G-6 duties include the scheme of signal support with PACE per phase, DODIN operations planning, SATCOM, line of sight and tactical radio planning, spectrum management and COMSEC account management [S6 2-10]. Battalion S-6 duties include supportability of each COA, the PACE plan, spectrum coordination with the brigade spectrum manager and the COMSEC local hand receipt [S6 2-29 to 2-30]. The theater army G-6 has a COMSEC account manager, a spectrum element, a cybersecurity branch and a joint and coalition network section that writes the information management plan [S6 2-50]. The Annex H appendixes are 2 Network Operations, 3 Network Diagrams, 4 Satellite Communications, 5 Foreign Data Exchanges, 6 Spectrum Management and 7 Information Services [S6 Table B-8]. HIGH

### 7. Canadian doctrine and organization (public level)

- **Planning.** CFJP 5.0 OPP has five stages: initiation, orientation, COA development, plan development and plan review [S11 chapter 4]. Staff analysis is done branch by branch, "for example J1, J2, J3, J4, J5, and J6" [S11 3-12]. The standard annex list puts Communications and Information Systems in Annex Q, and paragraph 5 Command and Signal may refer to the CIS annex [S11 5E-3, 5E1-2]. CF OPP is moving toward AJP-5 alignment (sibling note 05, Summary item 3). HIGH
- **CIS doctrine.** No public CFJP 6.0 was found (searches of publications.gc.ca and the web). AJP-6 is the obvious public substitute because Canada is a NATO nation and CAF planning is aligning with NATO doctrine (sibling note 05). MEDIUM
- **Cyber force construct.** CAF Cyber Command was established on 26 September 2024 as "the single DND/CAF authority for cyber operations" [S14]. It reports directly to the CDS, is administered by the Digital Services Group, and integrates force employment, generation and development of cyber forces including SIGINT and joint EW, supporting the move to a pan-domain battlespace [S15]. Its units include CFS Leitrim (SIGINT), the Canadian Forces Electronic Warfare Centre, the Canadian Forces Network Operations Centre ("enterprise cyber defence and force generation") and the Cyber Training Unit [S16]. HIGH
- **Authorities.** CAF offensive cyber operations are "approved by the Government on a mission-by-mission basis" under domestic and international law; CSE's 2019 CSE Act mandate includes defensive and active cyber operations, which need a Ministerial Authorization from the Minister of National Defence, with consultation of (defensive) or request or consent from (active) the Minister of Foreign Affairs [S17]. MEDIUM (official page read through an extraction)
- **Enterprise IM/IT.** The Digital Services Group was formed on 1 May 2024 from the Chief Information Officer Group and the Digital Transformation Office, led by the ADM (Digital Services) and Defence Chief Digital Officer, with a mandate that includes IM, digital services, and cyber security and operations [S18]. MEDIUM
- **Deployed J6 support.** The Canadian Forces Joint Signal Regiment (Kingston) provides CIS that "enables effective command and control of task forces deployed on continental and expeditionary operations", under the Canadian Forces Joint Operational Support Group, which takes direction from CJOC [S20]. The Canadian Joint Response Force HQ is CJOC's deployable joint headquarters [S21]. Canadian Joint Forces Command (established 25 November 2025) leads joint capability development, including joint doctrine; the release does not describe a force employment role [S19]. MEDIUM
- **Five Eyes and NATO alignment.** Canada sits on the CCEB with the US [S1 II-7], so ACP-based procedures are shared ground for a Canadian profile. HIGH

### 8. NATO C3 alignment (AJP-6 Edition B, AJP-3.20)

- **J6 role.** "the J6 staff should lead the planning, coordination, and execution of CIS architectures and joint operations area CIS"; J6 planners, with the cyberspace security element, prepare recovery and consequence management plans, assess adversary impact on coalition CIS, and take part in producing the JRFL "through the Theatre Spectrum Management elements inside J6, under the responsibility of the J3"; in some nations EW planning is done by J6 planners [S12 1.14c to 1.14e]. HIGH
- **Federation.** FMN is "the preferred way to achieve interoperability"; every participant manages its own portion of the mission network under rules from the network management authority; a lead nation may run a common services hub at corps and division in large-scale combat; mission network CIS should reflect the C3 taxonomy and be cyber defence compliant [S12 1.3j, 1.15a]. HIGH
- **Planning products.** Strategic: CIS assessment, CIS estimate, CONOPS CIS guidance, CIS support plan. Operational: CIS assessment and estimate, IERs (Annex Q to the CONOPS) and a CIS service matrix (Annex Q to the OPLAN). CIS planning runs in step with J2, J3 and J5 [S12 3.2 to 3.4]. HIGH
- **Cyberspace.** CIS operations "are an integral part of cyberspace operations" [S12 1.1c]. The NATO Cyberspace Operations Centre coordinates NATO cyberspace operations; OCO goes through SCEPVA, and command of the contributing forces stays national [S13 chapter 1 section 3 p. 11, 2.23]. HIGH (AJP-3.20 2020 text)

### 9. Draft team file (operational variant, three of ten members)

This shows the shape only; the full file should follow the same pattern for every member in Table 5.

```markdown
---
name: J6 C4 and cyber planning team
description: Drafts the communications system estimate, the communications annex, PACE plans and J6 inputs to spectrum and cyber planning. Exercise data only. Humans decide.
privacy: private
---

## j6-lead
role: J6 planning chief; integrates the estimate and the annex
model: deep
tools: Read, Glob, Grep, Write, Edit, TodoWrite, Remember, Recall, CreateDocument, CreatePresentation
privacy: private
Your area is J6/ESTIMATE.md, J6/ANNEX.md and J6/HANDOFFS.md. Read the doctrine profile first and use its annex letter and role codes. Work through the five planning areas: mission analysis, information requirements, interoperability and supportability, capability, allocation. Each member writes its own section file; you merge them. End the estimate with two plain statements: whether the mission is supportable from a J6 view, and which COA is best supported, with reasons. You recommend; you never approve, authorize, assign or release. Anything that needs a decision goes in a "Decision needed by <human role>" list. Do not accept or write real IP addresses, frequencies, call signs, key dates or classified markings; stop and tell the user. Done when ESTIMATE.md and ANNEX.md exist, every section has an owner, and the j6-checker has reviewed them.

## spectrum-manager
role: J6 spectrum manager (J-6 inputs to JEMSOC and JFMO)
model: standard
tools: Read, Write, Grep, Recall
reports-to: j6-lead
privacy: private
Your area is J6/SPECTRUM.md. List EMS-use requirements by net and system, host nation limits, and the J-6 input to the JRFL using TABOO, PROTECTED and GUARDED only as categories. The JEMSOC (normally in the J3) and the JFMO own the JRFL and the JEMSO appendix; you write inputs for them. Use exercise frequencies only. Done when SPECTRUM.md lists every requirement with owner, location, time and the gap it leaves.

## cyber-defense-planner
role: DODIN operations and DCO-IDM planner
model: deep
tools: Read, Write, Recall
reports-to: j6-lead
privacy: private
Your area is J6/CYBER-DEFENSE.md. Map mission objectives to the networks and services they depend on (mission-relevant terrain in cyberspace), rank the key terrain, and write preplanned responses for restoration and intrusion. Ask the J2 team for the network threat summary. You do not plan offensive or response actions outside our own networks: write any such need as "Requirement for J3 cyberspace planner: effect needed and why", with no technique, target or access detail. Done when CYBER-DEFENSE.md has the key terrain list, the preplanned responses and the J3 requirement list.
```
