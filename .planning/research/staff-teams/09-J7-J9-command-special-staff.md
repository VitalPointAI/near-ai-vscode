# Research note 09: J7, J9, and the command group with the personal and special staff

> **Edition update (2026-11-18).** This note used JP 5-0 of 1 December 2020. JP 5-0 of 1 July 2025 supersedes it and was read from the owner's reference copy for design note 10. JPP step names are unchanged; US Joint COA tests are now suitable, feasible, acceptable, distinguishable, complete; the fourth planning function is plan evaluation; annex I is Knowledge, Information, and Data Management and annex K is Command, Control, Communications, Computer, and Cybersecurity Systems. See design note 10, sections 2.6, 2.8 and 2.9. CADN 13-01 (2013): religious leader engagement is chaplain ministry, not intelligence collection or an influence activity, so the chaplain support member takes no collection or influence tasks.

Date: 2026-11-18. Scope: doctrinally correct agent team templates for the NEAR AI VS Code extension covering the J7 (joint force development: training, exercises and lessons learned), the J9 (civil-military operations, CIMIC and interagency), and the command group with the personal and special staff. The templates must work at strategic, operational and tactical levels under US and Canadian doctrine. Note 06 (full joint staff) covers the whole staff structure; this note goes deep on these roles.

Method: public, unclassified sources only. Doctrine was read in full text where a public copy was available. The Joint Staff and several military hosts block automated download, so some US training and lessons publications (CJCSM 3500.03F, CJCSI 3150.25H, CJCSM 3150.25C, JP 3-57) were seen only through official listings and search excerpts; those claims are marked MEDIUM or LOW. Every claim carries a source tag such as [S6 2-107]. Each tag resolves in the Sources section to a title, publisher, date and URL. Confidence: HIGH means the primary text was read; MEDIUM means an official secondary source, an older edition, or an excerpt; LOW means a catalogue, directory or unreviewed listing. Text marked "Proposal" is this note's design advice, not doctrine.

Related notes: [00-bastion-staff-teams.md](00-bastion-staff-teams.md) (Bastion's J7, J9 and special staff agents; the extension's team file format), [01-agent-exchange.md](01-agent-exchange.md) (partner MCP controls), [02-J2-intelligence.md](02-J2-intelligence.md) (format this note follows), [03-J3-operations-J4-logistics.md](03-J3-operations-J4-logistics.md) (battle rhythm, assessment cell), [05-J5-plans.md](05-J5-plans.md) (JPP and OPP crosswalk; its legal-adviser seat and assessment planner hand off to the team proposed here).

## 1. Summary

1. **Several US publications were renumbered or rewritten. Cite the current numbers.** JP 1-04 was renamed and renumbered **JP 3-84, Legal Support** (2 August 2016) [S28]. JP 1-05 was replaced by **JP 3-83, Religious Affairs in Joint Operations** (19 December 2022) [S37]. **CJCSM 3500.03F**, the Joint Training Manual, was issued 8 August 2025, "significantly rewritten", and replaces the old JTIMS with the **Joint Training Tool** [S7]. JP 3-33, Joint Task Force Headquarters (31 January 2018) is the main public source for every role in this note [S1]; it was listed as under revision in 2021 [S2], so its currency should be rechecked before shipping.
2. **J7 and J9 are optional directorates at a JTF.** A J-7 is "normally formed for a standing JTF" [S1 XI-1]. A J-9 is one option among several: a J-code directorate, a branch inside the J3 or J5, staff spread across existing sections, or a JIACG [S1 XIII-3]. At tactical level the US Army puts training under the G-3 or S-3 [S6 2-56] and has a required G-9 or S-9 from brigade combat team upward [S6 2-73]. The templates must therefore offer a "standalone section" and a "cell inside another team" variant.
3. **The J7's work is a closed loop: requirements, plans, execution, assessment, then lessons.** The US Joint Training System runs requirements, plans, execution and assessment [S8]. Exercises follow a life cycle of design, planning, preparation, execution and evaluation, with concept, initial, middle, MSEL and final planning conferences and an after action review [S9, LOW]. The Joint Lessons Learned Program has five phases: discovery, validation, resolution, evaluation and dissemination [S1 XI-3][S10]. The CJTF assigns a lesson manager and a JLLIS administrator [S1 XI-4].
4. **The MSEL is the J7's key exercise product and must trace to objectives.** It is "a chronological timeline of expected actions and scripted events to be injected into exercise play by controllers". It "ensures necessary events happen so that all objectives are met" [S12]. Proposal: every inject row carries the training objective it tests, and a gate rejects injects that test nothing.
5. **Canada has a defined lessons process but a thin public JTF J7 picture.** DAOD 8010-0 makes Commander CJOC responsible for the lessons learned program, requires the steps in CFJP A2 Lessons Learned (now under review), and requires use of the Defence Lessons Learned System (DLLS) [S15][S16]. CJOC, through the Canadian Joint Warfare Centre, is the Designated Training Authority for joint individual training, including joint lessons learned [S17]. CFJP 5.0 gives the plan an **Annex BB, Training and Exercises**, and issues the termination order only after lessons data is collected [S19 1-7, 5E1-2]. No public source found describes a CAF JTF J7 billet; treat that as a gap.
6. **US CMO and NATO or Canadian CIMIC are not the same frame.** The US JTF J-9 handles "all matters concerning CMO and interorganizational coordination", writes **Annex G (Civil-Military Operations) and Annex V (Interagency Coordination)**, and gives CMO input to targeting "to reduce destruction of essential civilian capabilities" [S1 XIII-4 to XIII-5]. NATO defines CIMIC as a joint function with two core activities, **civil factor integration** and **civil-military interaction**. NATO's ACOS J9 is the coordinating authority over CIMIC assets, and the CIMIC concept of operation is "typically **Annex W**" [S25 pp. 2, 11, 17, 22]. The CAF plan format also puts CIMIC at **Annex W** [S19 5E1-2]. Canadian CIMIC doctrine (1999) was "primarily based on NATO CIMIC doctrine" [S26].
7. **The command group decides; agents only support.** The commander supervises all planning and keeps the establishing authority informed [S1 II-8 to II-9]. The deputy may chair boards and assume command [S1 II-10]. The COS is "the key JTF staff integrator and synchronizer" and runs the battle rhythm [S1 II-10 to II-11][S5]. The CSEL is "a key advisor to the commander, often serving as a sounding board" [S1 II-11]. In Canada "the OPP belongs to the Commander: the Chief of Staff (COS) and G5 manage it" [S20 p. 12], and the COS orchestrates the decision brief [S19 4-11]. The DND/CAF AI strategy requires AI-augmented decisions to be justified, documented, traceable and explainable [S47]. Proposal: no member of any team in this note may approve, release, sign, or speak for a person.
8. **Legal and medical support agents must say plainly that they support qualified people.** The SJA's advice "should not be filtered through an intermediary" [S1 XIV-4 to XIV-5], and CAF commanders get LOAC advice from OJAG legal advisors [S30 ch. 2]. The WHO warns that large models can produce "false, inaccurate, biased, or incomplete" statements and that users may hand hard decisions to the model through automation bias [S49]. The ABA applies the existing duties of competence, confidentiality and supervision to lawyers who use generative AI [S48]. Section 3.7 gives the exact sentences the instructions must carry.
9. **Some roles do not travel across nations.** The US JTF special staff has an Inspector General, the "eyes, ears, voice, and conscience of the CJTF" [S1 XIV-13]. Canada has no statutory inspector general; oversight relies on internal review and the Ombudsman [S45, LOW to MEDIUM]. US Army doctrine lists the safety officer as personal staff [S6 2-129] and has no named protocol officer; protocol sits with the aide-de-camp and the secretary of the general staff [S6 2-117, 2-130]. The CAF senior non-commissioned adviser is a chief warrant officer, not a CSEL [S40][S42].
10. **Three proposed teams, all `privacy: private`.** A **J7 team of 8**, a **J9 team of 8**, and a **command group and special staff team of 10** with two swap-in members (safety officer and protocol officer). Each team runs in its own lane in parallel with the J2, J3, J4 and J5 teams and hands products across through files. Every team has a blocking reviewer gate. Section 2, Tables 12 to 15 give the details.
11. **Data access splits cleanly.** Public: humanitarian and disaster data (ReliefWeb, HDX, FTS, GDACS), law texts (DoD Law of War Manual, Canadian LOAC manuals, ICRC IHL databases), public affairs feeds (DVIDS API), exercise templates (HSEEP). Commercial: media monitoring and legal research databases. Partner only: JLLIS, the Joint Training Tool, DLLS, the NATO Lessons Learned Portal, medical intelligence, and command SharePoint or equivalent [S10][S7][S16][S13]. None of the MCP servers named here has been reviewed or run.

## 3. Details

### 3.1 J7 under US doctrine

**Place in the HQ.** The J-7 "assists the commander in planning and executing the joint training program for the staff and theater assigned forces", and it is "an optional directorate normally formed for a standing JTF" [S1 XI-1]. The CCDR defines readiness criteria for JTF-capable headquarters, and those criteria shape the HQ's joint training [S1 XI-1]. Training is individual (preparing people for joint duties and systems) or collective (preparing staffs and teams to integrate and synchronize capabilities) [S1 XI-1]. The J-7 sends personnel requirements to the J-1 and requests extra capabilities through the J-3 RFF manager [S1 XI-1]. The commander and COS set the J-7's structure; the notional one has training and education branches [S1 XI-1 to XI-2]. HIGH.

**Responsibilities.** Prepare training guidance for the commander's approval; identify training requirements from the mission; determine and allocate training resources; maintain each unit's readiness status; use Joint Knowledge Online or equivalent; fold functional training into individual and collective training; and evaluate continuity training objectives to operate "under denied or disrupted conditions" [S1 XI-2]. Training areas for the JTF staff include individual military skills (CBRN, health protection, force protection), functional orientation, HQ situational awareness, JOA culture and customs, and mission-focused training and exercises [S1 XI-3]. HIGH.

**Joint Training System.** CJCSM 3500.03F (8 August 2025) is the current Joint Training Manual. It was "significantly rewritten", replaced the legacy Joint Training Information Management System with the Joint Training Tool, and removed procedural descriptions [S7]. Earlier public descriptions give four JTS phases: requirements (producing the joint mission-essential task list), plans (joint training plans linking requirements to events), execution (training events and proficiency observations), and assessment (training proficiency and mission training assessments that feed future plans) [S8]. The template should use these names but label them "per CJCSM 3500.03 series; confirm terms in 3500.03F". MEDIUM.

**Exercise life cycle and the MSEL.** A CJCS notice describes the joint exercise life cycle as design, plan, preparation, execution and evaluation, with a concept development conference, initial planning conference, middle planning conference, MSEL conferences, final planning conference, and after action review [S9, LOW]. The public HSEEP doctrine gives a usable, fully public model of the same pattern. It defines the MSEL as "a chronological timeline of expected actions and scripted events to be injected into exercise play by controllers to generate or prompt player activity", which "ensures necessary events happen so that all objectives are met" and "links simulation to action" [S12]. HSEEP also defines the MSEL meeting, the controller and evaluator handbook, exercise evaluation guides and control staff instructions [S12]. NATO's exercise process is set out in Bi-SCD 075-003 (1 September 2023) with a stage 0 initiation annex and exercise control staff roles [S14, LOW]. HIGH for HSEEP, LOW to MEDIUM for the joint and NATO process names.

**Joint Lessons Learned Program.** The JLLP's "primary objective is to enhance joint force readiness and effectiveness" through improvements across DOTMLPF-P, in five phases: discovery, validation, resolution, evaluation and dissemination [S1 XI-3][S10]. The CJTF "will assign a lesson manager and Joint Lessons Learned Information System (JLLIS) administrator"; the lesson manager tracks, coordinates and evaluates lessons through all phases [S1 XI-4]. CJCSI 3150.25H (30 December 2021, Change 1 of 5 April 2024) is the policy; CJCSM 3150.25C (23 June 2023) is the procedure manual [S10][S11]. JLLIS is the DoD system of record and needs a CAC [S10]. Other staff sections feed lessons: the SJA must "capture and share joint legal LL" [S1 XIV-6], and the safety officer collects and disseminates "joint safety lessons identified" [S1 XIV-22 to XIV-23]. HIGH for JP 3-33, MEDIUM for the CJCS documents.

### 3.2 J7 under Canadian and NATO doctrine

**CAF lessons learned policy.** DAOD 8010-0 (issued 30 April 2004, modified 22 July 2016, reviewed 13 March 2026) commits DND and the CAF to a lessons program "that results in tangible changes in attitudes, capabilities and behaviors" [S15]. Commander CJOC oversees the program's development and approves its guidance; L1 advisers and commanders of commands implement it; the steps in B-GL-025-0A2/FP-001, CFJP A2 Lessons Learned (under review), must be followed; and managers and commanders must use the Defence Lessons Learned System to capture observations and lessons identified [S15]. DLLS is "the pan-DND/CAF Lessons Learned system of record", available on DWAN or CSNI, with planned AI features for proofreading, transcription and similar-item recommendations [S16]. The definition of a lesson learned used in Canadian orders is "a lesson identified for which validated remedial action has been implemented" [S18 via the DAOD; MEDIUM]. HIGH.

**CAF joint training.** Since 2014 CJOC has delivered joint individual training through the Canadian Joint Warfare Centre. In February 2020 Commander CJOC became the Designated Training Authority for joint operations individual training, with focus areas of joint lessons learned, joint modelling and simulation, joint targeting and joint ISR [S17]. HIGH.

**CAF plans and the JTF.** CFJP 5.0 puts training into the plan as **Annex BB, Training and Exercises**, and records as **Annex CC, Documentation, Records and Reports** [S19 5E1-2]. The termination order is issued only when the operational commander is satisfied that all activities are complete, "including such activities as the post-operation accounting and the collection of data for the Lessons Learned process" [S19 1-7]. Lessons learned are one of the factors the staff analyse in COA development [S19 4-8][S20 Stage 3 factors]. No public source read describes a JTF J7 billet in the CAF; this note treats the J7 functions as present (annex, LL collection, CJOC program) and the billet as a gap. MEDIUM.

**NATO lessons process.** The NATO LL Handbook (4th edition, June 2022) describes two phases, analysis and implementation, with the steps plan, observe, analyse, decide, implement and validate, and share. It starts from the commander's priorities and a lessons collection plan [S13 pp. 17 to 18]. The Joint Warfare Centre applies the process to NATO exercises (Annex C of the handbook) [S13 p. 17]. AJP-5 Edition B states that capturing lessons from operations and exercises "is the responsibility of every level of command" [S22 1.22]. HIGH.

**CAF planning is changing.** The Canadian Forces College plans to start teaching AJP-5 as the joint operational planning process in the 2026/2027 academic year, and the Canadian Army will replace its OPP with NATO APP-28 by August 2026 [S21]. Templates should offer a CFJP 5.0 profile now and an AJP-5 profile next (note 05 covers the crosswalk). HIGH.

### 3.3 J9 under US doctrine

**Purpose and placement.** The J-9 "is an optional directorate the CJTF may establish to assist in the pursuit of unified action by facilitating the integration of interorganizational perspectives in planning, execution, and assessment" [S1 XIII-1]. Partners "are most often not under US military command authority", so success depends on coordination mechanisms and shared understanding [S1 XIII-1]. Alternatives to a J-code directorate are a branch in the J-3 or J-5, staff spread across sections, or a JIACG [S1 XIII-3]. The J-9 is the staff entry point for external mechanisms such as a CMOC, an embassy country team, or the UN cluster system; CMOCs are "increasingly rare at the operational, JTF level" because host nation or UN centres overshadow them [S1 XIII-3]. HIGH.

**Responsibilities.** JP 3-33 lists 19 typical tasks [S1 XIII-4 to XIII-5]. The ones that shape the template: include interorganizational perspectives in OPTs, assessments and commander briefs; keep a close relationship with the POLAD's office; run an interorganizational coordination WG; build relationships with higher HQ, component and private sector counterparts; consider multinational partners' equities; write Annex G and Annex V; give CMO input to targeting; plan CMO along five LOEs; continuously assess the civil component; and oversee the CMOC [S1 XIII-4 to XIII-5]. The J-9 must also work closely with the SJA "to ensure CJTF meets numerous legal and regulatory obligations" [S1 XIII-4]. HIGH.

**Interorganizational cooperation.** JP 3-08 (12 October 2016, validated 18 October 2017) says joint operations are "integrated at the strategic level and coordinated at the operational and tactical level" with government agencies, IOs, NGOs, host nation agencies and the private sector [S23 executive summary]. It quotes the State Department's 3D Planning Guide: "to achieve unity of effort, it is not necessary for all organizations to be controlled under the same command structure", and lists four attributes for unity of effort: common vision, common understanding of the environment, coordination of efforts, and compatible measures of progress [S23 I-5]. These four attributes are a good structure for the stakeholder-mapper's coordination matrix (Proposal). HIGH.

**CMO doctrine.** JP 3-57 (9 July 2018) provides joint doctrine to plan, conduct and assess CMO. It consolidated the former civil affairs publication into JP 3-57, modified the definition of the CMOC, and added an appendix on civil information management [S24]. Its full text could not be read by tool, so the template should quote JP 3-33 and FM 6-0 for duties and cite JP 3-57 only as the reference. MEDIUM.

**Tactical level.** The G-9 or S-9 is "required at all echelons from a brigade combat team through theater army levels". It evaluates civil considerations in mission analysis, provides "actionable civil information", recommends a CMOC, prepares the groundwork for transition to civilian control, prepares **Annex K (Civil Affairs Operations)**, and coordinates Annex V with the G-3 [S6 2-73 to 2-75]. HIGH.

### 3.4 J9 under NATO and Canadian doctrine: CIMIC and the whole-of-government approach

**NATO CIMIC.** AJP-3.19 Edition B (June 2025) defines CIMIC as "a military joint function that integrates the understanding of the civil factors of the operating environment and that enables, facilitates and conducts civil-military interaction to support the accomplishment of missions and military strategic objectives in peacetime, crisis and conflict" [S25 p. 2]. It focuses on the operational level but applies at strategic and tactical levels [S25 p. xvi]. HIGH.

- **Levels.** The strategic HQ translates CIMIC policy into doctrine and training and holds liaison authority with non-military actors. At operational level the commander is advised by the ACOS J9, who "acts as the coordinating authority over CIMIC assets across all components" and trains staff. At tactical level CIMIC "conducts and facilitates interaction with regional and local military and non-military actors" [S25 p. 11].
- **Core activities.** Civil factor integration identifies, analyses and assesses civil factors "to contribute to the decision-making process"; civil-military interaction covers activities between NATO bodies and non-military actors "to foster mutual understanding" [S25 p. 17].
- **Staff activities.** Civil factor integration uses PMESII and ASCOPE; the CIMIC estimate "is a continuous process"; staff activities include identifying complementary non-military actions, assessing the impact of military operations on non-military audiences, identifying resilience shortfalls, advising on synchronization, developing the CIMIC concept of operation (typically Annex W), establishing information-sharing mechanisms, and assessing requests for military support [S25 pp. 21 to 22]. Civil-military interaction covers outreach, engagement, consultation and collaboration, and civil-military liaison, with liaison principles of right level and competence, continuity, mutual exchange of information, and avoidance of duplication [S25 pp. 22 to 24].
- **Comprehensive approach.** NATO's comprehensive approach combines all instruments of power, and CIMIC "plays a key role in synchronizing military and non-military activities" [S25 pp. 2, 5]. HIGH.

**Canadian CIMIC.** The CAF's joint CIMIC manual, B-GG-005-004/AF-023 (15 January 1999), is written at the operational level, applies to domestic and international operations, and is "primarily based on NATO CIMIC doctrine" with US civil affairs and UK experience [S26]. CFJP 5.0 requires the task force commander to establish "workable relationships with government, civil agencies and the civil population" as early as possible, with concerns including supply, facilities, services, labour and the movement of civilians and refugees [S19 3-3]. The plan's CIMIC annex is Annex W [S19 5E1-2]. Because Canadian doctrine follows NATO and the CAF is moving to AJP-5 [S21], the CAF profile of the J9 team should use AJP-3.19 terms (CFI, CMI, CIMIC estimate, ACOS J9) and cite the 1999 manual as the national basis. MEDIUM (age of the Canadian manual).

**Whole-of-government.** CFJP 5.0 starts strategic planning with a "Whole of Government/Strategic Assessment" and says that "whether termed 'whole of government' or a 'comprehensive approach'", civil and military activities "must be integrated into a coherent, synergistic whole" and built into the campaign plan [S19 1-11, ch. 5]. CFJP 01 (2009) provides the CF doctrinal basis for interdepartmental and interagency action under these concepts [S27]. In a non-combatant evacuation, once the Government of Canada approves it, the CAF deploys a scaled JTF to coordinate its activities in support of Global Affairs Canada and the Canadian head of mission [S63]. Proposal: the stakeholder-mapper's CAF variant lists other government departments first, then IOs, NGOs and host nation actors. MEDIUM.

### 3.5 The command group

**Commander.** The CJTF's 18 listed duties include determining C2 relationships, supervising all planning, establishing policies, exercising risk management, keeping the establishing authority informed, coordinating with forces and agencies not assigned (including governments, NGOs and IOs), and establishing succession of command [S1 II-8 to II-9]. In the Canadian OPP the commander amends or approves the restated mission and CCIRs and issues planning guidance at the mission analysis brief [S20 Stage 2 table], and "the Commander may abbreviate or modify the OPP" [S20 p. 12]. The CCIRs are the commander's: the commander "focuses the staff by designating certain information as critical" [S1 IV-15]. HIGH.

**Agents never decide.** This is both doctrine and policy. Doctrine puts decisions with the commander [S1 II-8][S20 p. 12]. The DoD adopted five AI ethical principles in February 2020, including that AI be responsible, traceable and governable [S46]. The DND/CAF AI Strategy (March 2024) requires that AI decisions that augment human judgement be justified and documented, with final decisions traceable and explainable and backed by accountability measures [S47]. Proposal for the decision-support-officer: (a) present options, never a selection; (b) state the staff recommendation as the staff's, attributed to the person who owns it; (c) record decisions only from a person, with name, time and verbatim wording; (d) refuse to mark any product "approved". HIGH for sources; the rules are a design recommendation.

**Deputy.** The DCJTF is usually from a different service, may serve as COS, is the principal assistant, and may represent the CJTF, assume command, chair designated boards, and supervise designated planning or units [S1 II-10]. In the Army the commander designates the second in command and may delegate authority for specific functions [S6 1-29 to 1-30]. Proposal: no deputy agent; board support goes to the decision-support-officer. HIGH.

**Chief of staff as synchronizer.** "As the key JTF staff integrator and synchronizer, the COS establishes a framework of trust, shared understanding, and intent within the staff" through staff processes, capacity management, priorities, knowledge sharing and IM [S1 II-10]. The COS coordinates and directs staff work, supervises estimates, plans and orders, manages the battle rhythm and assigns a battle rhythm manager, implements policy, ensures liaison, supervises staff training and integration, and supervises the joint manning document [S1 II-11]. The J7 focus paper adds that the COS is "the staff integrator and synchronizer to support the commander's decision-making requirements", should "synchronize versus control staff efforts", should establish regular command group synchronization meetings, and should manage staff bandwidth [S5 pp. 1 to 3]. The commander drives the TOR "while the COS has responsibility for its production and dissemination" [S4 p. 2]. The COS appoints the IMO [S1 IV-15]. A second edition of the COS paper (January 2020) exists [S5 note]. The COS is the strongest fit for agent support in this note, because most of the work is coordination, tracking and assembly. HIGH.

**CSEL or CWO.** The CSEL is "a key advisor to the commander, often serving as a sounding board" and "a trusted observer"; duties include advising on enlisted personnel, monitoring discipline, morale and readiness, being a command spokesperson, reviewing documents, developing NCOs, and liaising with multinational contingents [S1 II-11 to II-12]. The traditional HQ structure is "Commander, Deputy(s), Command Senior Enlisted Leader (CSEL), Chief of Staff, personal and special staffs, and J-staff" [S4 p. 2]. The Army CSM "drives the force by transmitting and translating the commander's intent" [S6 1-31]. In Canada the CAF Chief Warrant Officer, also called the senior enlisted leader, is the principal non-commissioned adviser to the CDS [S40], and command teams of commander and chief warrant officer "typically function at the tactical and operational levels" [S42]. Proposal: an enlisted-perspective-advisor reviews drafts through the CSEL's or CWO's lens; it never speaks for that person. MEDIUM for Canadian sources.

### 3.6 Personal and special staff, role by role

**Legal: SJA (US) and LEGAD (CAF).** JP 1-04 was renamed and renumbered JP 3-84, Legal Support, on 2 August 2016 [S28]. JP 3-33 sets out the role in detail. The SJA is the principal legal adviser, should have direct access, and "their advice should not be filtered through an intermediary" [S1 XIV-4 to XIV-5]. Duties include ensuring all plans, ROE, RUF and directives conform with the DoD Law of War Program and law; drafting Appendix 2 (Legal) to Annex E; assisting Appendix 7 (ROE/RUF) to Annex C as a member of the ROE WG; reviewing the plan "for legal sufficiency"; reviewing target and weapon plans as a JTCB member; and coordinating ROE training [S1 XIV-5 to XIV-6]. The legal estimate covers authorities and limitations, references and agreements, law of war, human rights, claims, contracts, HNS, fiscal and environmental law, SOFAs, incident investigations, detainees, intelligence oversight and more [S1 XIV-7 to XIV-8]. The DoD Law of War Manual (June 2015, updated July 2023) is public [S29]. In Canada, commanders "must comply with LOAC and ensure that those they command comply", and "Legal Advisors (LEGADs) from the Office of the Judge Advocate General (OJAG) provide advice and support to CAF commanders on the application of LOAC" [S30 ch. 2]. Commanders should understand that allies may be party to different LOAC treaties [S30 ch. 2]. CFJP 5.0 asks for early consultation with legal staff on LOAC, SOFAs and HNS, and its ROE procedures are in B-GJ-005-501/FP-000 [S19 3-3]. Deployed CAF legal officers in Afghanistan supported targeting, detention and rule of law issues [S32]. HIGH for JP 3-33 and the 2025 handbook.

**Why legal-support drafts and never concludes.** Doctrine makes legal sufficiency and target legal review the SJA's or LEGAD's professional responsibility [S1 XIV-6][S30 ch. 2]. Professional rules apply existing duties of competence, confidentiality and supervision to lawyers using generative AI [S48]. The member therefore issue-spots and researches with full citations, and the human legal officer decides.

**POLAD.** The POLAD advises the commander on foreign policy issues and "does not have the responsibility or capacity to represent the broader DOS portfolio" to the staff [S1 XIII-4]. The POLAD is a personal or special staff member "as directed" [S1 II-17]. A State Department officer serves as a civilian foreign policy adviser to a military command [S39]. POLADs and cultural advisers "often can provide invaluable insights" and are often by-name requests [S4 p. 2]. In the CAF OPP, political considerations are a staff factor and a political adviser can brief at mission analysis [S20 Stage 2 table][S19 4-8]. Note 05 folds POLAD-style analysis into its strategy-policy-planner; Proposal: when both teams run, the command group's polad-support owns the political-military assessment and note 05's planner consumes it. MEDIUM.

**PAO.** PAOs "participate throughout the JPP", "aggressively anticipate and counter enemy propaganda, putting accurate information out first", and advise the CJTF "on potential implications of operational decisions on public perception" [S1 XIV-2]. PA plans must match the plan phases and coordinate with the host nation, country team, agencies and NGOs [S1 XIV-2]. PA and other information capabilities "differ with respect to audience, scope, and intent and must be coordinated and synchronized" [S1 XIV-3]. Annex F holds the PA support; the CJTF designates a security review officer for imagery [S1 XIV-3 to XIV-4]. JP 3-61 (17 November 2015, with Change 1) is the PA doctrine [S33]. In Canada the DAOD 2008 series governs PA, and DAOD 2008-4 covers PA, military doctrine and CF operations [S34]; CFJP 5.0 says PA requirements "are an integral part of the process" and puts PA in Annex X [S19 3-3, 5E1-2]. HIGH for JP 3-33, MEDIUM for the others.

**Chaplain.** Religious affairs comprise religious support and religious advisement. The chaplain advises on "all matters of religion, morals, ethics, quality of life, and morale" and the "ethical/moral implications of plans, policies, and operations"; prepares religious support portions of plans; and, when directed, conducts religious liaison "consistent with their noncombatant status", advising the JFC on "the constraints and restraints as defined by the noncombatant status of chaplains" [S1 XIV-9 to XIV-10]. JP 3-83 (19 December 2022) replaced JP 1-05 [S37]. The Royal Canadian Chaplain Service advises commanders "on spiritual, religious, moral and ethical issues affecting Canadian Forces personnel and their families during operations", and QR&O chapter 33 governs chaplain services [S38]. Proposal: the agent may describe religious demography, calendars, sites and impacts; it must not provide pastoral care, must not touch confidential counselling, and must not produce anything that uses religious leaders as intelligence sources or targets. HIGH for JP 3-33.

**Surgeon.** The JTF surgeon is "the CJTF's principal advisor for joint FHP and HSS and reports directly to the CJTF", assesses requirements and capabilities, and coordinates Annex Q [S1 XIV-11 to XIV-12]. JP 4-02 (11 December 2017, Change 1 of 28 September 2018) is the detailed source [S35]. In the CAF, task force surgeons work in daily contact with home health services operations [S36], and HSS is Annex K [S19 5E1-1]. Proposal: the agent works with population-level public data (WHO, travel health notices, disaster alerts), never with individual health records, and never gives clinical advice. MEDIUM for Canadian sources.

**Inspector General.** The IG is "an extension of the eyes, ears, voice, and conscience of the CJTF", "a candid broker and an impartial fact finder", whose basic functions are to "inspect, assist, and investigate" [S1 XIV-13]. The IG must "work for and have access to the CJTF and all elements" [S1 XIV-14]. Canada has no statutory inspector general; proposals for one were not enacted, and oversight uses internal review and the Ombudsman [S45]. Proposal: exclude the IG from default templates. IG work handles complaints, protected disclosures and investigations about individuals, and an agent would weaken the confidentiality and independence that make the office work. LOW to MEDIUM for the Canadian point.

**Knowledge management.** The COS appoints the IMO, who writes the IM plan, captures information exchange requirements, publishes the IMP or SOPs and chairs the JIMB [S1 IV-15]. The J7 focus paper says IM focuses on tools and KM on people, organization and process; "KM is the supported function and IM is the supporting function"; and the KMO, guided by the COS, keeps the battle rhythm focused on the knowledge the commander needs [S4 p. 13]. The Army KMO writes the KM plan, works with the G-6, and is "not required by doctrine or regulation to prepare an annex" [S6 2-103]. JP 5-0 lists Annex I (Knowledge and Information Management) [S3]. Proposal: km-officer also keeps the command chronology and document collection for the historian [S1 XIV-21]. HIGH.

**Operations research and analysis.** The Army ORSA officer "conducts analysis in support of operations across staff sections", is "the principal advisor to the COS and the commander on analytical techniques", is often the action officer for the assessment WG, assists with Annex M, supports COA analyses and provides quality control [S6 2-107 to 2-108]. Joint assessment cells have data management, survey and analysis cells; analysts need critical thinking, quantitative and qualitative methods, and "basic mediation skills" [S1 IX-13 to IX-14]. In Canada, DRDC's CORA provides operational research to CJOC through an annual work program and runs analytical wargames [S43]. Note 03 and note 05 also have assessment roles. Proposal: the assessment-analyst does the analysis and quality control; the J5 assessment planner owns the assessment plan and Annex N. HIGH for FM 6-0.

**Protocol.** No protocol officer appears in JP 3-33 or in the FM 6-0 lists read. The Army aide-de-camp coordinates protocol activities, and the secretary of the general staff directs DV itineraries and monitors official social events and ceremonies [S6 2-117 to 2-118, 2-130]. Canada publishes an official Table of Precedence [S44]. Proposal: protocol-officer is a swap-in for strategic HQ teams. HIGH.

**Safety officer.** "Safety is a command responsibility." The JTF safety officer develops mishap prevention, cross-flows safety information, collects joint safety lessons, tracks mishap investigations and corrective actions, gives "an unbiased perspective of the safety climate", reports Class A to D mishaps and trends, and puts emphasis on a "worst hazard first" basis [S1 XIV-22 to XIV-24]. In the Army the safety officer is personal staff and prepares Appendix 2 (Risk Management) of Annex E (Protection) [S6 2-129, 2-142]. Proposal: swap-in for tactical teams and for every live exercise the J7 designs. HIGH.

### 3.7 Human authority and the advice disclaimers

Proposal. These sentences go verbatim into the instructions of the named members, and the reviewer gate checks that every output carries them.

**legal-support (every output, first line):**
"Draft for review by a qualified legal officer (SJA or LEGAD). This is research and drafting support, not legal advice. It does not replace the judgement of a qualified legal officer, who must review it before anyone relies on it. It may be incomplete or wrong. The commander's legal adviser advises the commander directly."

**health-services-support (every output, first line):**
"Draft for review by a qualified medical officer. This is planning support, not medical or clinical advice. It does not replace the judgement of a qualified clinician, who must review it before anyone relies on it. It uses population-level public data only. It may be incomplete or wrong."

**Every member of all three teams (closing rule):**
"You support people; you do not decide, approve, release, sign, task people, or speak for anyone. When a decision is needed, write it as a decision for a named role (commander, COS, SJA or LEGAD, surgeon, PAO, exercise director) and stop that line of work."

Basis: SJA advice goes directly to the commander [S1 XIV-5]; LEGADs advise CAF commanders [S30 ch. 2]; the WHO warns of false or incomplete outputs and automation bias [S49]; the ABA applies existing professional duties to generative AI use [S48]; and the DND/CAF strategy requires traceable, accountable decisions [S47].

### 3.9 Pitfalls in detail

1. **Stale and renumbered references.** JP 1-04 is now JP 3-84 [S28]; JP 1-05 is now JP 3-83 [S37]; JTIMS was replaced by the Joint Training Tool [S7]; CFJP 5.0 is being replaced by AJP-5 for joint operational planning [S21]. JP 3-33 (2018) was under revision in 2021 [S2]. Templates must show the edition they cite.
2. **Agents that decide.** Doctrine reserves decisions to the commander [S1 II-8][S20 p. 12], and both the US and Canadian AI policies require accountable, traceable human decisions [S46][S47]. The risk is quiet: a decision brief that says "the staff recommends COA 2" can drift into "COA 2 is approved" in a later product. The decision-log MCP should require a named human decider for every entry.
3. **Legal and medical outputs read as professional advice.** Large models can produce "false, inaccurate, biased, or incomplete statements", and users may over-trust them through automation bias [S49]. Legal professional duties still apply to AI-assisted work [S48]. Section 3.7's disclaimers and the "never conclude" rules are the mitigation.
4. **Filtering the SJA.** JP 3-33 says the SJA's advice "should not be filtered through an intermediary" [S1 XIV-5]. If the decision brief summarizes legal-support's draft as "legal advice", the agent has become exactly that intermediary. The decision-support-officer must cite the human legal officer's own words or mark the legal line "pending SJA or LEGAD".
5. **Mixed national formats.** US joint plans put CMO in Annex G and PA in Annex F; the CAF puts CIMIC in Annex W, PA in Annex X, medical in Annex K and legal in Annex AA [S1][S19 5E1-1 to 5E1-2]. Army tactical orders use Annex K for civil affairs operations [S6 2-75], which collides with the CAF's Annex K (HSS). A NATION and LEVEL setting must drive every annex letter.
6. **CMO and CIMIC confused; J9 assumed to exist.** The US frame is CMO plus interorganizational coordination [S1 XIII-4]; NATO and the CAF use CIMIC with CFI and CMI [S25]. The J9 is optional and may be a cell in the J3 or J5 [S1 XIII-3]. Offer both variants.
7. **Implying command over partners.** Partners "are most often not under US military command authority" [S1 XIII-1], and unity of effort does not require a single command structure [S23 I-5]. Products that "task" NGOs or IOs damage relationships. The partner-perspective-reviewer checks wording.
8. **Civilian personal data.** Mishandled humanitarian data can endanger people already at risk; OCHA's Data Responsibility Guidelines (October 2021) cover safe, ethical and effective data management [S50]. Civil registers must hold no personal data.
9. **Real-world material in exercises.** Exercise scenarios must be fictional and marked. Compiling unclassified items can create classified information; DoD information security policy addresses classification by compilation [S51]. The J7 reviewer and the J9 reviewer both run a compilation check.
10. **MSEL drift.** HSEEP says the MSEL ensures necessary events happen "so that all objectives are met" [S12]. Injects that test nothing waste the training audience's time; objectives with no inject go untested. The exercise-workbench trace check catches both.
11. **Lessons that are never learned.** A lesson identified becomes a lesson learned only after remedial action is implemented and validated [S13][S18]. Agents should keep a status ladder (observation, LI, action proposed, action tasked, implemented, validated) and only people move items past "action proposed".
12. **PA blended with influence.** PA and other information capabilities differ in audience, scope and intent and must be coordinated, not merged [S1 XIV-3]. The public-affairs-planner must not draft deceptive or influence content.
13. **Chaplain's noncombatant status.** Religious liaison must be consistent with noncombatant status [S1 XIV-10]. The religious-affairs-support member must not produce targeting, collection or influence products about religious leaders.
14. **Automating the IG.** The IG's value is impartial fact-finding and confidential assistance [S1 XIV-13]. Excluded by default.
15. **False precision.** ORSA work brings "mathematical rigor" [S6 2-107], but decision matrix weights are judgements. Show sensitivity and uncertainty and name who set the weights.
16. **Duplicated ownership across teams.** See Section 3.8. One owner per product, everyone else hands off.
17. **Labels are not accreditation.** As note 02 records, the extension's labels are an organizational control and not accreditation, and clearances are self-declared [S62 note 02]. Templates stay unclassified, and classified material must never be loaded.
